🌧️ AquaGrow

Intelligent rainwater allocation under water scarcity — a simulation-based study comparing allocation strategies for multi-zone irrigation

Python Algorithm Design Sensitivity Analysis 3 Experiments

Research Question

How should limited harvested rainwater be allocated among competing irrigation zones under different levels of water scarcity, priority differences, and rainfall uncertainty?

AquaGrow is evaluated as one allocation strategy among several, not a predetermined winner — the goal is to find the conditions under which each strategy actually performs best.

Three Strategies Compared

  • Baseline — fixed-threshold, fixed dose, first-come-first-served
  • Fair-share — equal proportional split among needy zones, no priority or rain awareness
  • AquaGrow — priority-weighted allocation (moisture deficit × crop priority) with a rain-signal reserve

Experiment 1 — Scarcity Curve

Question: How does demand satisfaction change as tank capacity increases, for each strategy? Tested 10L–150L, 40 independent 30-day scenarios per capacity per strategy.

Fallback: Mean Demand Satisfaction increases with Tank Capacity. Fair-share and AquaGrow closely match, reaching ~98.8% at 150L, while Baseline plateaus near ~22%.
Fair-share and AquaGrow track each other closely across the entire range, both dramatically outperforming Baseline at every capacity. Baseline never exceeds ~22% satisfaction even with abundant water, because it only ever serves whichever zone is checked first and ignores the rest.

Experiment 2 — Priority Sensitivity

Question: Does AquaGrow's priority-weighting help the highest-priority zone when the weight gap is made more aggressive? Tested at 40L tank, 40 scenarios per configuration. Weight configurations were fixed before running the experiment, not tuned afterward to force a result.

With the original mild weights, AquaGrow showed limited advantage over Fair-share for Zone A (45.0% vs 40.4%). But with a wider, deliberately more aggressive weight spread (P4), a clear trade-off emerges: Zone A's satisfaction climbs from 40.4% to 57.7%, while overall fairness drops from 49.1% to 43.7%. This confirms the earlier modest result wasn't a flaw in the algorithm — the original weights just weren't strong enough to produce a pronounced redistribution.

Experiment 3 — Rainfall Probability & Reserve Strategy

Question: Given a simulated rain signal calibrated to a known daily rain probability, what reserve fraction performs best?

Honesty note: this models an environment with a fixed daily rain probability and a signal calibrated to that same probability — it is not a real weather forecasting system. It tests reserve strategy, not forecast skill.

"Best reserve" is defined here as the reserve fraction producing the highest mean demand satisfaction in this experiment.

The most important discovery in this project: reserving water never helps, at any rain probability tested. Satisfaction monotonically decreases as reserve fraction increases, in every scenario. Rainfall that arrives tomorrow adds to the tank regardless of whether water was reserved today — reserving doesn't protect anything, it only withholds water from zones that need it right now. This reveals a genuine conceptual flaw in the reserve mechanism as originally designed, not a tuning problem.

Overall Conclusions

  • Intelligent splitting matters enormously — both Fair-share and AquaGrow vastly outperform fixed-threshold Baseline at every scarcity level tested.
  • Priority-weighting requires a strong enough signal to matter — mild weights show little differentiation; aggressive weights produce a real, quantifiable trade-off between prioritizing high-value zones and system-wide fairness.
  • The current rain-reserve mechanism is actively counterproductive and should be redesigned or removed.

Rather than claiming AquaGrow is simply "better," this project identifies specifically when priority-weighting helps and specifically where the original design fails — backed by 40+ scenario-runs per condition rather than single seeded results.

Directions for Further Work

  • Redesign the rain-reserve mechanism so it actually provides a benefit (e.g. conditioning on anticipated demand spikes, not simply "rain might come")
  • Extend the priority sensitivity test across multiple tank capacities
  • Add real historical rainfall data for a specific location instead of a probability-based synthetic model
